What’s new for Spring 2027

A redesign built around one idea: capability you can prove.

The program has been rebuilt end to end — fewer documents, more human expertise, and every deliverable pointed at the same target: a graduate who can build with AI, reason about it at institutional scale, and tell that story to anyone.

New structure

The Rehearsal Ladder

One story, told to four progressively harder audiences — an open IU-wide AI workshop, a solutions architect met twice, real users in testing, then the public. Every rung prepares the next, so by showcase night the pitch has already been shaped by three rooms of feedback.

New

Solutions architect consults

Two 45-minute sessions with UITS engineers who ship enterprise AI — feasibility at Week 5, scale and pitch rehearsal at Week 9.

New

A self-guided learning pathway

Teams use AI to map their idea’s three hardest scale concerns, then design their own curriculum to close the gap.

Redesigned

A Kickstarter-style pitch video

Show it working, then make the ask — the single most shareable portfolio object the program produces.

Redesigned

One living brief, not thirteen documents

Thirteen Word documents collapse into one versioned brief, assessed at three checkpoints.

Redesigned

Assessment measures capability

Does the artifact work? Is the reasoning sound? Is the reflection honest? The document is no longer the grade.

New

Three experts, defined lanes

Campus mentor, teaching & learning mentor, solutions architect — with a published “who to ask what” map.

New

Campus equity guardrails

Story outweighs production value, phone-only production is first-class, and expert meetings default to virtual.

New

AI disclosure & media standards

Stated norms for disclosing AI-generated media, on-camera releases, and consent for voice and likeness.

The experience

Real problems. Real users. Real rooms.

Ambassadors don’t complete exercises — they work the way well-resourced institutions actually work on hard problems, and they leave with the evidence.

Real problems, real consequences

Every project starts from a genuine student or university problem, gets tested with real users, and is shaped side by side with people who ship software for 90,000 students. The question is never “is it done?” — it’s “would IU adopt this?”

AI as a lifelong learning instrument

Prompting techniques expire; the ability to use AI to map an unfamiliar problem space and then deliberately close your own knowledge gaps doesn’t. The learning pathway makes that skill explicit, practiced, and assessed.

Authentic AI systems thinking

Security and data classification, accessibility review, cost in year three, governance after graduation, obsolescence when the next model ships — students learn how institutions actually decide, from the people who decide.

Media production, with judgment

Working alongside IU’s video and advanced visualization team, teams put AI inside a real production workflow — scripting, storyboarding, b-roll, captioning — and learn where AI helps and where the human is still better.

A portfolio that does the talking

A working public artifact, a two-minute pitch video, a showcase page — objects a student can drop into LinkedIn, a personal site, or an interview. Links, not claims.

Reflection that goes deep

Weekly reflection discipline, plus a structured post-expert reckoning: what did we think was hard, what was actually hard, and why didn’t we see it? Blind spots get named, not performed — across every discipline in the cohort.

The spine of the program

Progressive authentic collaboration with users and experts.

The hardest professional skill is explaining your work to someone who doesn’t already care. Ambassadors get four real repetitions — and every rung is preparation for the next.

1

Weeks 2–4 · Low stakes

The workshop conversation

Share the work with IU students, faculty, and staff at an open, IU-wide UITS-led AI workshop. Two conversations, one actionable takeaway — the first time the idea leaves the team.

2

Weeks 5 & 9 · High stakes

The solutions architect

One expert, met twice. Ideate on feasibility together at Week 5 — expert feedback while there’s still time to act on it — then reunite at Week 9 to think bigger: scale, marketability, the pitch itself.

3

Week 6 · The artifact speaks

Real users, testing

Three to five real users try the artifact while the team watches. This audience doesn’t hear the pitch — the thing either works for them or it doesn’t.

4

Weeks 9–10 · Permanent

Pitch video & showcase

Persuade an audience that owes you nothing — in public, on the record, in a video that lives on your portfolio forever.

The ten weeks

A semester, told as a story.

Scroll through the arc of the program — from a blank problem statement to a public showcase.

01

Week 01 · Foundation

The ladder is named.

Teams form, roles lock, and working norms get written down. Each team drafts a problem definition rooted in real student or university life — and learns, explicitly, that everything ahead is one story told four times to progressively harder audiences.

  • Team roles assigned — coordinator, project lead, research, implementation, quality
  • Working norms and workspace setup; problem definition statement drafted
  • Training Module 1: how these systems actually work
Training M1
02

Week 02 · Users & research

Meet the people you’re building for.

Ten student interviews per team turn a hunch into evidence. The workshop window opens — the first rung of the ladder — and the media capture habit begins: ten minutes a week that will save the pitch video seven weeks from now.

  • Student interviews and user insight synthesis
  • Workshop conversation window opens (rung 1)
  • Weekly media capture habit begins — photos, clips, failures
  • Training Module 2: beyond prompting — context engineering
Training M2 Ladder rung 1 opens
03

Week 03 · Concept & design

Commit to one idea worth building.

Concepts are explored, one is locked, and the prototype workflow takes shape. Teams analyze three reference pitch videos — why do they work? — and the self-guided learning pathway begins: what would break this idea at university scale?

  • Solutions exploration; concept locked; prototype workflow designed
  • Pitch inspiration analysis — three references, deconstructed
  • Learning pathway begins: three enterprise scale concerns, case-study grounded
  • Training Module 3: beyond chat — tools, retrieval, agents
Training M3 Brief checkpoint 1
04

Week 04 · Build

Make the thing exist.

Prototype v1 comes to life, with prompt experiments documented as real intellectual work — first answer, why it was useless, what changed, where it got sharp. The workshop conversations are due: two conversations across the IU AI community, one actionable idea.

  • Prototype v1 built; prompt experiments logged in the archive
  • Workshop requirement due — conversation log submitted
  • Training Module 4: knowing whether it works — evaluation and test cases
Training M4 Rung 1 complete
05

Week 05 · Feasibility

Trade ideas with someone who ships.

The week the training was built for. A one-page brief lands 48 hours ahead, then the team sits down with an enterprise solutions architect to collaborate: pressure-test the design, trade ideas, and surface IU capabilities that could fit — feedback that arrives while there’s still time to act on it. Afterward, the reflection: what did we think was hard, and what did the expert see?

  • Prototype testing and refinement
  • Architect brief due 48 hours prior — no exceptions
  • Architect meeting #1: feasibility, direction, what IU capabilities could fit
  • Pathway reflection: the prediction-versus-reality gap, named
  • Training Module 5: enterprise reality — identity, data, procurement, cost
Training M5 Ladder rung 2
06

Week 06 · User testing

Watch a real user try to use it.

Three to five real participants, a testing plan, and the humbling footage of someone getting stuck. The mid-sprint demo is internal and deliberately rough — “here’s what we have, tell us what’s broken” — the first of three registers every team learns to speak.

  • User testing with 3–5 participants; findings into the brief
  • Mid-sprint demo — internal, rough on purpose
Brief checkpoint 2 Ladder rung 3
07

Week 07 · Dark week

Nothing is due. On purpose.

Placed on the shared campus break, the dark week is a design decision, not a gap. Real projects have pauses; pretending otherwise just moves the chaos. For campuses whose breaks don’t align, one rule holds everywhere: no new deliverables due.

  • No deliverables. Rest is part of the calendar, not a failure of it.
Designed pause
08

Week 08 · Refinement & ethics

Make it right, not just real.

Testing findings drive refinement. Ethics, accessibility, and responsible use get written into the living brief as engineering constraints, not afterthoughts — including the program’s AI disclosure standards for anything the team publishes. Pitch pre-production begins with a library of captured footage, not a blank timeline.

  • Refinement from user-testing findings
  • Ethics, accessibility & responsible-use review into the brief
  • Pitch pre-production — script, storyboard, format choice with a named audience
Ethics & accessibility
09

Week 09 · Pitch & scale

Show it working. Then make the ask.

Pitch video production with AV support — AI in the workflow where it genuinely helps, humans where they’re better. Then back to the architect, with a different job this time: not “will it work?” but “where does this go, and who would want it?”

  • Pitch video production — Kickstarter-style or vertical, chosen rhetorically
  • Architect meeting #2: scale, marketability, pitch rehearsal
  • How AI is reshaping enterprise software development itself
Rung 2 · second meeting Pitch video
10

Week 10 · Showcase

The top of the ladder.

The final artifact goes live at a public link. The showcase tells the how — process, evidence, what failed, what was learned — to an audience of funders, mentors, and employers. Every ambassador leaves with a portfolio that speaks for itself and a story about being wrong that only gets better in interviews.

  • Final artifact published — a working public link
  • Showcase presentation and showcase page copy
  • Exit interview, contribution summary, resume & LinkedIn guidance
Brief checkpoint 3 Ladder rung 4

What a graduate can do

Six sentences an employer can verify.

  1. Judge where generative AI genuinely helps a problem — and where it does not.

  2. Build and iterate a working AI artifact, and evaluate whether it actually works.

  3. Use AI to map an unfamiliar problem space and deliberately close their own knowledge gaps.

  4. Anticipate what an institution needs before adopting their work — security, cost, accessibility, maintenance, obsolescence.

  5. Explain and sell that work to peers, experts, and the public — adjusting register to audience.

  6. Point to a public artifact, a pitch video, and a specific story about what they got wrong and what they learned.

The program as a whole

A selective, paid, ten-week applied AI internship open to students on every Indiana University campus. Teams of four to five identify a real problem, build something that addresses it with generative AI, test it with real users, develop it alongside enterprise practitioners, and pitch it publicly.

Cohort & identity

  • ~50 students per semester across all nine campuses; ~11 teams of four to five.
  • Spring and fall cohorts — approximately 100 students per year.
  • Competitive application, with campus distribution an explicit selection goal.
  • Paid, 5–7 hours per week, ten weeks including one designed dark week.
  • Team roles carried forward unchanged: Team Coordinator, Project Lead, Research/Data Lead, Implementation Lead, Quality/Review Lead — each member holds one primary and one backup role.
  • Two project tracks: an AI-powered artifact (a tool, assistant, or workflow) or an AI resource (a guide, prompt library, or tutorial). Track determines the expert pairing and testing protocol.

The four support relationships

Published to students and mentors as a “who to ask what” guide, so advice never collides.

Assessment model

What is assessed and the criteria used
ObjectCriteria
The artifactDoes it work? Can an unfamiliar user succeed with it? Problem fit.
Living briefSoundness of reasoning at three checkpoints.
Learning pathwaySpecificity of concerns; defensibility of source selection; honesty of the blind-spot reflection.
Pitch videoProblem clarity; credibility of the ask; story. Production value is explicitly not weighted.
IndividualReflection logs and contribution summary.

Program-level: after each meeting, architects submit a two-line debrief — what did this team most misunderstand, and what should they have known? Aggregated across the cohort, that produces a ranked list of what novices systematically fail to anticipate, and the next cohort’s training curriculum writes itself from it.

Roughly 15–20 UITS staff per semester at 3–8 hours each — a bounded, specific ask, which is what makes it sustainable. Student wages are the dominant budget line: ~100 students per year at 45–63 paid hours each.

What carries forward unchanged

The strongest material in the existing program is retained deliberately: team roles with primary and backup assignments, working-norms design, the Sprint Projects 101 framing, professional feedback practice, Microsoft Teams as system of record, ChatGPT Edu Projects as the development workbench, weekly reflection discipline, the campus mentor rhythm, Kuali time entry, the existing GenAI Student graphics, and the exit interview with resume and LinkedIn guidance.
